Prompt Intelligence

We do not guess the questions. We find them.

02For whom
Persona-Based Prompting

One question. Six buyers. Six different answers.

“Which phone should I buy under ₹30,000?” is not one prompt. Asked by a first-time buyer in a tier-2 city, an upgrader switching platforms, and a creator who shoots handheld, it returns three different shortlists — and your brand appears in some and not others.

We run each prompt through a matrix of your ICPs, then report visibility per persona. The gap between your best and worst persona is usually larger than the gap between you and your nearest competitor.

Defensibility

Two of these five, nobody else builds the same way.

Audit, fact checking and content recommendation are competitive capabilities — done well, they win on execution, and a rival with a better dashboard can take them. The first two cannot be taken that way. One needs a conversation corpus and an adjudication process behind it; the other needs a prompt set generated per buyer rather than one set filtered by audience. Both change the input, which is why the other three return a different answer here than anywhere else.

Proprietary · IP-ready

Persona-matrix prompting

The market reports one visibility number per brand, and a few tools will now slice that number by audience. We do something different: we rewrite the question itself for each buyer and score every variant separately, because a first-time buyer and a replacement buyer do not ask the same question and will not get the same shortlist.

The size of the effect is what makes filtering insufficient. Conditioning one question on a different buyer swaps up to 75% of the recommended set. A segmented view of one shared prompt set reports the same prompts through a different lens; it cannot surface a shortlist that only exists when the question is asked differently. The published audit is explicit that aggregating across personas hides the finding — which means a stable average is not evidence of stability.

We recommend daily, including on Measure. Answer engines are probabilistic — the same prompt put to the same engine twice does not reliably return the same answer. A weekly reading gives four samples a month per prompt; a daily reading gives thirty. Thirty samples separate a real movement in your position from ordinary run-to-run variance. Four frequently cannot, which means a weekly-only programme can report a change that was never there, or miss one that was.
